For every $100 of revenue in its latest quarter, Nvidia kept $66 as operating profit. Equinix kept $23. CoreWeave lost $2.

Everyone is building AI data centres. The question I keep asking: who actually keeps the margin — the chipmaker, the GPU cloud, or the landlord?

I went through the latest filings. Three findings:

1. The chip layer keeps most of it. Nvidia's quarter to July: revenue $96.2B, data centre $89.0B, gross margin 75.0%. It guides 74% for the quarter to October.

2. The landlord earns a steady, utility-like margin. Equinix's Q3 revenue was $2.57B, up 11%, with a 51% adjusted EBITDA margin and record bookings of $533M, up 35%.

3. The GPU cloud in the middle is where it gets squeezed. CoreWeave's Q2 adjusted EBITDA margin was 59%, almost the same as Equinix. But after depreciation it posted a 2% operating loss, and net interest of $640M was about a quarter of its $2.58B revenue. Backlog is about $104B, so demand is not the issue. The cost of capital is.

Why this matters for the Gulf:

HUMAIN is building in the two layers with the thinnest margins: the buildings and the GPU cloud. Its first 14 MW of Blackwell systems deploy from Q4 2026, with 70 MW of Vera Rubin planned from mid-2027, 50 MW of it for a US client.

The levers it has are exactly the lines that hurt CoreWeave: power and capital. Saudi cloud operators pay 18 halalas per kWh (about $0.048), a tariff approved in 2021. And HUMAIN's funding this year is mostly equity and state-backed debt: SAR 9.9B ($2.6B) in cash from Aramco, and up to SAR 2.5B ($667M) of Infra facilities signed this week.

Cheap power and patient capital don't change Nvidia's share of the bill. They change whether the middle layer can make money.

What I'll watch next: Nvidia's results on 17 November, especially gross margin against its 74% guide, and whether CoreWeave's operating margin turns positive.

If you're building or financing compute in the region, which line do you think decides it: power, chips, or the cost of capital?
